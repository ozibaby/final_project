##Introduction

Some college majors are studied mostly by women, others mostly by men. This analysis asks a simple question: Do majors with more women in them lead to lower pay?

##Description of the Data

The data comes from FiveThirtyEight’s The Economic Guide To Picking A College Major, built from the American Community Survey 2010–2012 Public Use Microdata Sample. The file is available at https://github.com/fivethirtyeight/data/tree/master/college-majors.

Each row is one undergraduate major not a person. Each row tells you what a typical graduate of that major earns, not what any particular graduate earns. The dataset covers 173 majors across 16 broad fields like engineering, health and education.

##Data Analysis

Before comparing groups, here is what these majors look like overall (Table 1). Each number describes the median of all of them.

So the typical major is 53 percent women dominated, pays about $36,000 USD a year, and has about 6.80 percent of its graduate out of work.

Majors were sorted into two groups based on the share of graduates who are women. Where women make up less than half the major is counted as ‘Male Dominated’, where they make up half or more, it is counted as ‘Female-dominated’.

Looking at it this way, we see the two groups earn very different amounts, majors where women make up at least half of graduates report substantially lower median earnings (Table 2).

Among women dominated majors, the typical graduate earns about $34,000 USD.

In majors where men dominate, it’s about $45,000 USD a substantial difference.

The unemployment row is almost identical between the two groups. Graduates of women-dominated majors find jobs just as easily, they simply get paid less for them.

The regression uses the actual percentage and asks how much earnings change as the share of women goes up.(Table 3).

For every one percent of women in a major, the typical pay changes by $-304 USD.

Each dot in (Figure 1) is one major, the pattern as you move right towards majors with more women, the dots drift downward.

##Conclusion

There is a clear pattern here, but a pattern isn’t a cause. This analysis cannot tell us why majors with more women pay less, it could be various reasons which the data cannot separate.

It is also important that pay here covers everyone who graduated from a major, men and women together. The data does not report what men and women earn separately. So this is not a finding about women being paid less than men for the same work. It is a finding about which fields pay well and who ends up in them.

Finally, each row is a major, not a person, so nothing here describes what any individual graduate earns.
